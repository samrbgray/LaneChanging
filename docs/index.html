Context

Autonomous Vehicles & the Road Ahead

Autonomous and semi-autonomous vehicles are entering the public roadway network faster than our understanding of their behavior can keep up. Systems like Tesla Full Self-Driving (FSD) and Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC) represent the forefront of consumer-available partial automation — capable of controlling steering, acceleration, and braking under certain conditions, yet still requiring human oversight.


As of 2024, approximately 5 million Tesla vehicles operate on roads globally, with roughly 500,000 equipped with FSD capability. These transitional autonomous vehicles (tAVs) are not a future scenario — they are already a significant and growing share of the everyday vehicle fleet, sharing lanes with human drivers who were never trained to interact with them.

SAE Automation Levels


L0 No automation — human controls all tasks
L1 Driver assistance — one function (e.g. ACC)
L2 Partial automation — steering + acceleration (Tesla ACC/Autopilot)
L3 Conditional automation — system drives; human must be ready (Tesla FSD)
L4 High automation — no human needed in defined scenarios
L5 Full automation — no human required under any conditions

tAVs in this dataset operate at Levels 2–3

The Problem

Why Lane Changing is Hard to Study

Lane changing is among the most complex driving maneuvers in mixed traffic. It is a multi-agent interaction requiring real-time gap acceptance, coordination with surrounding vehicles, and split-second decision-making. When a tAV performs a lane change — or responds to one — its behavior can differ meaningfully from a human driver, creating interaction dynamics that are not yet well understood.

Safety


tAV lane-change decisions may create unexpected safety-critical situations for surrounding human-driven vehicles, particularly during gap acceptance under tight spacing.

Traffic Flow


Differences in tAV deceleration profiles, gap preferences, and response times can propagate upstream as stop-and-go waves, affecting throughput even at low market penetration rates.

Data Gap


Existing lane-change datasets were collected before tAVs entered the market. Empirical, naturalistic data with cm-level precision is needed to calibrate next-generation behavioral models.

The Dataset

What NC-tALC Provides

The NC-tALC dataset provides 152 controlled lane-changing trials — 72 LC experiments and 80 Responding experiments — collected on Sunset Lake Road in Apex, North Carolina. Each trial captures a complete lane-change event from a four-vehicle instrumented platoon operating in real mixed traffic.


Two experiment types are included: one in which a tAV acts as the lane changer, and one in which tAVs act as responding follower vehicles. Both Tesla FSD and ACC driving modes are represented across a range of downstream spacings and desired vehicle gap configurations.


All trajectory data is captured at 20 Hz with cm-level RTK-GPS accuracy. Each trial also includes forward-facing video, CAN bus signals, and energy consumption data.

  • RTK-GPS positional trajectories at 20 Hz (0.01 m accuracy)
  • Speed, heading, roll, and pitch at high frequency
  • Forward-facing video at 1920×1080 / 30 fps
  • CAN bus data via ScanMyTesla (FSD & ACC modes)
  • Energy consumption per trial
  • Pre-computed lane distance columns (lateral & longitudinal)
  • Vehicle type labels (LV, LCV, F1, F2) per row
  • Relative longitudinal distance from lane-change event
